## Geometry of surfaces and amigurumi

Amigurumi style crochet is characterised by stacking rows of double crochet stitches directly on top of each other. Often worked in cotton, the resulting fabric is dense and close, holding its own shape. This makes it particularly well-suited to geometric analysis. Indeed, a piece of crochet is a polygonal mesh of a surface in 3 dimensional space, naturally parametrised by row and stitch counts. On the other hand, if we begin with a parametrised surface, a crochet pattern can be generated through a process of discretisation. 

In computer graphics, surfaces are typically rendered as meshes, for ease of storage and computation. Many geometric objects such as the Lapalace-Beltrami operator and Gauss curvature have discrete analogues developed for the purpose of analysing these meshes, and we can do the same for crochet.

### Increases, decreases and curvature
Anyone who has crocheted or knitted will appreciate that increases and decreases impact the curvature of the resulting fabric. The specific impact will depend on other factors such as stitch gauge and whether you work in rows or in the round.

The two patches are crocheted in UK double crochet (dc), with identical yarn, hook and tension, and with all stitches facing the same way (the crochet version of stockinette). For the overlayed graphs, every stitch gets a vertex, and two stitches are considered adjacent if they are next to one another on the same row, or if one stitch is worked into the top of the other. 

[](#fig:flat-crochet) contains no increases, and an example vertex is labelled $u$. [](#fig:inc-dec-crochet) has a 2dc increase on row 3 (vertex $s$) and a 1dc decrease on row 4 (vertex $t$). The overlayed graphs illustrate the effect of these increases and decreases on the degree of each vertex: $d(u)=4$, $d(s)=6$ and $d(t)=3$. 

The *voronoidal decomposition* of a triangle $T$ is defined as follows. Let $c(T)$ denote the centroid of $T$, that is, the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the edges. Depending on $T$, $c(T)$ may lie inside or outside of $T$. For each vertex $v$ of $T$, the associated voronoidal region is the quadrilateral with vertices $v$, $c(T)$ and the midpoints of the two edges incident at $v$:
:::{figure} figs/tri-voronoi.png
:label: fig:tri-voronoi
:width: 250

Triangle subdivided into voronoidal regions
:::

Note that if $T$ has an obtuse angle, then its voronoidal regions will not all lie strictly inside $T$.

::::{prf:definition} Mesh curvature
:label: def:-curvature
Let $M$ be a surface and $\Gamma$ a triangular mesh. Let $v$ be a vertex of $\Gamma$, and $T(v)$ the set of triangles incident at $v$. 

Consider the voronoidal regions associated with each triangle incident at $v$. Let $A(v)$ denote the combined area of these regions. Let $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_n$ denote the angles at $v$.

:::{figure} figs/v-voronoi.png
:label: fig:v-voronoi
:width: 300

Example: vertex $v$ with combined voronoidal region $A(v)$ and incident angles $\theta_i$.
:::

The *mesh curvature* of $\Gamma$ at $v$ is 
:::{math}
:enumerated: true
:label: eq:mesh-curvature
K(v) := \frac{1}{A(v)}\left(2\pi - \sum_{i=1}^n\theta_i\right)
:::
::::

By the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, $K(v)$ provides a good approximation of the Gauss curvature of the underlying surface. For how good, see .... papers .... 

Let us return to the crochet of [](#fig:flat-crochet) and [](#fig:inc-dec-crochet). Naiively, tension is uniform and all stitches are the same size. This "should" mean that the voronoidal areas for each stitch are equal, and so are the angles incident at each stitch. Of course, in reality crocheters know that the tension is always slightly different at sites of increase or decrease, although the effects of this are usually compensated for in subsequent rows and average out over time. We can discuss the limitations of this assumption more later. For now, we proceed.

From a purely combinatorial standpoint, if $v$ is a stitch (vertex) in the interior of a piece of arigurumi, its order will be
$$
d(v) = 4+\text{\#(next-row increases into $v$)}-\text{\#(next-row decreases over $v$)}
$$
In particular, when the work is flat, every vertex has order 4 and the angles around every vertex sum to $2\pi$ radians. Moreover, the voronoidal area is equal to $hw$, where $h$ and $w$ are the height and width of each stitch, respectively. 
:::{figure} figs/flat-graph.png
:label: fig:flat-graph1
:width: 400

Voronoidal region and angles for flat crochet (c.f. [](#fig:flat-crochet)).
:::

At a site of increase or decrease, the order $d(v)$ goes up or or down according to the number of stitches gained or lost, but the interior angles and voronoidal area contributed by each stitch do not change (much). This means that both the angle sum and combined voronoidal area will scale by a factor of $\frac{d(v)}{4}$. In the notation of [](#def:-curvature), 
$$
A(v) = \frac{d(v)hw}{4},\hspace{1em}\sum_i\theta_i = \frac{d(v)}{4}\cdot 2\pi, 
$$
and
:::{math}
:label: eq:K(v)-crochet
:enumerated: true
K(v) = \frac{2\pi}{d(v)hw}(4-d(v)) = \frac{2\pi}{d(v)hw}(\text{\#decreases}-\text{\#increases}). 
:::


:::{prf:example} 
In [](#fig:inc-dec-crochet), stitch $s$ is a site of increase by $2$, and has order $6$, while $t$ is a site of decrease by $1$, and has order $3$. So, 
$$
K(s) = -\frac{2\pi}{3hw} \hspace{1em} \text{ and } \hspace{1em} K(t) = \frac{2\pi}{3hw}
$$
(I did not intend $K(s)=-K(t)$ but it appears to have happened anyway.)
:::

### Crocheting in the round vs in rows

A lot of amigurumi begins by crocheting a small number of stitches into a chained ring or magic loop. This produces crochet in a slightly different configuration.

6dc into magic loop

If we want the work to continue to stay remotely flat, it is clear we will need to make significant increases in the next round. 

:::{figure} figs/6dc-12dc-round-graph2.png
:label: fig:6dc-12dc-round
:width: 400

So far so flat?
:::

For example, [](#fig:6dc-12dc-round) has 2dc worked into every dc of the first round, for a total of 12dc in round 2. The result appears flat, but as many a crocheter will know, it is rare for a work to stay that way. Trial and error increases produce gently rounded or overly wrinkly work, and though these effects can balance out, often the result is locally mishapen, detracting from the work's aesthetic appeal. 

Is there a reliable method to calculate the number of stitches required for a flat disc? Or indeed for any other specified curvature? 

Using the overlaid graph of [](#fig:6dc-12dc-round) and formula [](#eq:mesh-curvature), the central vertex has curvature
$$
K(v_0) = \frac{1}{A_0}(2\pi-6\theta)
$$
where $A_0$ is the combined voronoidal area shaded in dark blue. 

Assuming the initial round is functionally a flat disc, which it appears to be, we can probably safely take $\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}$ so that $K(v_0)=0$. 

What about subsequent rounds? We could insist that crochet is now indistinguishable locally from crocheting in rows, and try to apply [](#eq:K(v)-crochet). However, this would give for round 2, 

$$
K(v_{0j}) = \frac{2\pi}{d(v_{0j})hw}(4-d(v_{0j})) =-\frac{2\pi}{5hw} \neq 0.
$$

What is going on? In these low stitch count cases, a lot hangs on the proportions of the stitches themselves, and the fact they are not rectangles. More to the point, the space a single stitch occupies is different depending on how it is applied, since each stitch necessarily overlaps with other stitches.

Let's consider more closely the anatomy of (UK) double crochet stitches applied to a single dc on the previous row.

A UK double crochet (or US single crochet) ostensibly has 2 parts: a v part, which is always visible, and a top loop, which sometimes is partially or entirely covered by subsequent stitches. 

:::{figure} figs/loop-v.png
:label: fig:loop-v
:width: 300

Anatomy of a (UK) dc
:::

Technically, the loop part indicated in [](#fig:loop-v) is more closely attached to the stitch one to the right, but for the purposes of pattern organisation (especially in colour work) it is useful to group stitches this way.

On the top row, almost all of the stitches are visible. On other rows, the v's are always visible and (in theory) the same size. The top loops are more visible near decreases, and almost entirely covered near increases.

Suppose we have crocheted $n$ dc stitches into a magic ring. Then, if $r$ denotes the radius of the centre hole,

$$
\theta = \frac{a}{h+r} = \frac{b}{r} = 2\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{2\pi}{n},
$$

so  
:::{math}
:enumerated: true
:label: eq:theta-n
r = \frac{bh}{a-b}, \; \theta = \frac{a-b}{h}, \;\text{ and } \; n=\frac{2h\pi}{a-b}.
:::

:::{code-cell} python
:tags: remove-input
import numpy as np

dp = 4
a=1.2
b=0.3
h=1.3
w=0.7


theta = (a-b)/h

n0 = 2*np.pi/theta
n = int(round(n0))

r = (b*h)/(a-b)
:::

Moderately careful measurement suggests that for work in [](#fig:labelled-stitch) specifically, $a=$ {eval}`a`cm, $b=$ {eval}`b`cm, $w=$ {eval}`w`cm and $h=${eval}`h`cm. Substituting these into [](#eq:theta-n) gives $n=$  {eval}`n0`, which we round to $n=$ {eval}`n`. The centre hole radius is $r=$ {eval}`float(np.round(r,dp))`cm, and $\theta =$ {eval}`float(np.round(theta,dp))`cm. 

:::{figure} figs/9dc-round.jpg
:label: fig:9dc-round
:width: 400

{eval}`n`dc into a magic loop.
:::

On the other hand, for [](#fig:6dc-12dc-round), the central vertex $v_0$ has angle defect $2\pi - 6\theta = ${eval}`float(np.round(2*np.pi-6*theta,dp))` radians (positive curvature).


This suggests we missed the mark slightly with [](#fig:6dc-12dc-round). Of course in practice, brute force and ignorance will usually get you something flat enough to be getting on with, and if not, changing the hooksize will usually fix the issue.

But using this calculation will hopefully give a more naturally flat feel.

#### Round 2

Consider be a general round 2 stitch, $v_{0j}$, and suppose $k_{v_{0j}}$ is the number stitches we will crochet into $v_{0j}$ in round 3.


:::{figure} 
:label: fig:round2-measurements
:width: 400
:class: grid grid-cols-2 

![Round 2 crochet](figs/rnd2.png)

![More stitch dimensions](figs/labelled-stitch3.png)

Labelled stitch dimensiocalculationns for round 2.
:::

Elementary trig gives

1. $\frac{c+r}{w/2}=\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$, so $c=\frac{w}{2\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}-r$,

2. $d = \sqrt{r^2+(r+c)^2-2r(r+c)\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}$

3. $\sin\left(\frac{\theta_2}{2}\right)=\frac{w/2}{d}$, so $\theta_2 = 2\arcsin\left(\frac{w}{2d}\right)$

4. $\cos\alpha = \frac{w/2}{d}$, so $\alpha=\arccos\left(\frac{w}{2d}\right)$

For these particular measurements, we get

:::{code-cell} python
:tags: hide-input
c = w/(2*np.sin(theta/2))-r
d = np.sqrt(r**2+(r+c)**2-2*r*(r+c)*np.cos(theta/2))
theta_2 = 2*np.asin(w/(2*d))
alpha = np.acos(w/(2*d))

from tabulate import tabulate
tabulate([[a,b,c,d,w,h,theta,alpha,theta_2]],headers=["a","b","c","d","w","h","θ","⍺","θ₂"],tablefmt="html")
#print("2⍺+θ₂ = ",2*alpha+theta_2)
:::

(Note, $2\alpha +\theta_2 = \pi$.) 


The angle defect at $v_{0j}$ is
$$
2\pi - \left(\pi-\theta+2\alpha+k_{v_{0j}}\theta_2\right) = \pi +\theta - 2\alpha - k_{v_{0j}}\theta_2 = \theta +\theta_2 - k_{v_{0j}}\theta_2
$$

So for curvature $0$, on average, we would need $k_{v_{0j}}=\frac{\theta}{\theta_2}+1=$ {eval}`float(theta/theta_2+1)`.

So, stitch count for round 2 is approximately {eval}`n`$\times$ {eval}`float(theta/theta_2+1)` $=$ {eval}`n*(theta/theta_2+1)` $\approx$ {eval}`round(n*(theta/theta_2+1))`.
